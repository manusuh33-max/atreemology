Dasara or Dussehra? One root, two names, three myths

One festival. Two names. One Sanskrit root, and a few popular stories about it that aren't quite true.

Word Roots October 2026 6 min read

Both names grow from one Sanskrit word

Whether you grew up calling it Dasara or Dussehra, both names trace back to the same Sanskrit word: दशहरा, daśaharā.

दश daśa "ten"
+
हरा harā "that which takes away," from the verb root hṛ, "to take, carry off" — also behind Hara, Shiva as "the remover"
= "The one that takes away ten"

Ten what? Classical Sanskrit dictionaries like Apte and Monier-Williams answer: ten sins. Hold that thought, it's worth coming back to further down.

"Daśa" has cousins all over the world · déḱm̥

Sanskrit, Latin, Greek, and English all inherited their word for "ten" from the same shared ancestor language, reconstructed by linguists as Proto-Indo-European *déḱm̥.

Sanskrit
daśa
Dasara, Dashavatara, Dashamukha
Latin
decem
decimal, December, decimate
Greek
déka
decade, decathlon, decagon
Old English
tīen
ten, tenth, thir-teen

December was the 10th month of the early Roman calendar. Dashamukha, "ten-faced," is a name for Ravana — a word built from the very root this article is about.

So what actually changed between the two names?

Neither name is a mispronunciation of the other. Both are regular, separate sound changes applied to the same Sanskrit word as it passed into different modern languages.

ದಸರಾ

Dasara

Kannada · Telugu · Marathi

The "sh" softens to "s" and the middle "ha" drops out: daśaharā → dasarā.

दशहरा

Dussehra

Hindi · much of north India

Hindi drops the short middle vowel instead: daśaharā → daśharā. The "u" spelling is old British transliteration for a short "a," the same convention that gives us "Punjab."

Different sounds, same word, same meaning: "the one that takes away ten."

Same word, two very different stories

Here's where it gets interesting: the festival named by this one word is celebrated as two different victories, depending on where in India you are.

In Karnataka · Mysuru Dasara

Chamundeshwari's victory

Goddess Chamundeshwari slays the buffalo-demon Mahishasura, the demon Mysuru is believed to be named after. The festival has been held since 1610, begun by Raja Wodeyar I, and includes the Jamboo Savari elephant procession, Gombe Habba doll displays, and Ayudha Puja, where tools and vehicles are honoured.

In north India · Dussehra

Rama's victory

Lord Rama defeats Ravana, the ten-headed king of Lanka, and rescues Sita. Ramlila plays are staged through the nine nights, and giant effigies of Ravana, Kumbhakarna, and Meghnad are set alight at dusk, with fireworks and fairs across towns and cities.

Both land on the same day: Vijayadashami, from vijaya "victory" + daśamī "the tenth lunar day." Spot the daśa hiding in there too.

Myth 1: "Dussehra means Ravana's ten heads"

You've probably heard
"Dussehra = das + sir. It means Ravana's ten heads."
The root truth

दस + सिर (das + sir, "ten heads") is not where the word comes from. The real breakdown is दश + हरा (daśa + harā, "takes away ten").

There's no "head" anywhere in the word. The second half is harā, "taking away." Ravana's ten heads make a vivid story, but it's a popular reading, not what the word says, and it can't explain why Karnataka uses the exact same name for a festival that's about Chamundeshwari, not Ravana at all.

Myth 2: "Dasara is just a southern mispronunciation"

You've probably heard
"Dasara is just the south's way of mispronouncing Dussehra."
The root truth

Dasara and Dussehra are siblings, not parent and child. Each grew separately from the same Sanskrit दशहरा, daśaharā, through its own regular sound changes (see "So what actually changed," above). And Dasara isn't only southern: Marathi, spoken well north of Karnataka, calls it दसरा too.

Myth 3: "It's always been about Rama or Durga"

You've probably assumed
"The name has always been about Rama or Durga."
The root truth
हरते दश पापानि तस्माद्दशहरा स्मृता
harate daśa pāpāni tasmād daśaharā smṛtā
"It takes away ten sins, so it is called Daśaharā."
— Brahma Purāṇa 63.15

That verse isn't about autumn at all. It names the Ganga's festival, the bright tenth day of Jyeṣṭha (May–June), when she is said to have descended to earth. It's still celebrated separately today as Ganga Dussehra. The name daśaharā is older and broader than any one story told about it, which is exactly how it ended up attached to two unrelated autumn victories at once.
